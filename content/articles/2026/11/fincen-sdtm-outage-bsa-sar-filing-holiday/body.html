WASHINGTON — There is a dry, bureaucratic symmetry to watching America’s financial-intelligence agency fine a megabank more than a billion dollars for filing suspicious-activity paperwork late, and then — before the ink on the press release has properly cured — announcing that its own batch-filing inbox is down until December 30.

Under Order FIN-2026-R002, issued Nov. 24 pursuant to 31 U.S.C. § 5318(a)(7) and 31 C.F.R. § 1010.970, the Treasury Department’s Financial Crimes Enforcement Network granted “temporary, limited exceptive relief” after a service disruption crippled Secure Data Transfer Mode (SDTM), the automated batch pipeline that large financial institutions rely on to transmit Bank Secrecy Act reports. Every SDTM submission and Beneficial Ownership Information filing due between Nov. 18 and Dec. 29 now shares a single holiday-week catch-all deadline: Dec. 30, 2026.

An 866 Hotline for Urgent Money Laundering

One day later, on Nov. 25, staffs of the Federal Reserve Board, the FDIC, the OCC, and the NCUA released Supervisory Letter SR 26-10, assuring supervised institutions that examiners will neither cite a violation of law nor pursue enforcement actions over delayed SDTM filings during the outage. Banks remain legally obliged to keep monitoring suspicious transactions internally, of course — and if a compliance desk spots something genuinely urgent while the automated pipeline sits idle, FinCEN’s order helpfully supplies a toll-free telephone number.

“If a financial institution needs to expedite the delivery of suspicious activity information to law enforcement, filers can use the Financial Institutions Hotline, 1-866-556-3974, which is not impacted by this service disruption.” — FinCEN Order FIN-2026-R002 (Nov. 24, 2026)

The Infrastructure of Deterrence

No compliance officer should be penalized because the government’s own server stopped accepting uploads; extending deadlines during a federal outage is basic administrative sanity. Yet the spectacle underscores a quieter vulnerability in modern white-collar enforcement.

The entire architecture of American anti-money-laundering policing rests on high-volume automated data ingestion — millions of Suspicious Activity Reports and Currency Transaction Reports flowing continuously into a single Treasury bureau so investigators can trace cartel wires, sanctions evaders, and shell-company carousels before the money leaves the country. When that single front door goes dark for six weeks at the close of the fiscal calendar, the backlog does not merely wait politely on a compliance server until Dec. 30; it reminds every launderer on earth how thin the plumbing beneath the world’s largest financial cop really is.
