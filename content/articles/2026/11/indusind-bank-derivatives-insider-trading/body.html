MUMBAI — When a private-sector Indian lender discovers a roughly 2,000-crore-rupee discrepancy lurking inside its foreign-exchange derivatives book, prudence dictates an orderly accounting review and prompt disclosure to shareholders. According to India's market regulator, however, several senior executives at IndusInd Bank found time for an additional item on the agenda before the public was informed: selling their own shares.

In an interim order issued on May 28, 2025, the Securities and Exchange Board of India accused former chief executive Sumant Kathpalia, senior executive Arun Khurana, and three other bank officials of trading on unpublished price-sensitive information prior to the lender's public acknowledgment of a severe net-worth hit. SEBI froze 19.78 crore rupees — approximately $2.3 million — representing what the agency calculated as the losses the executives allegedly avoided by offloading stock ahead of the disclosure.

When the derivatives hole, estimated at roughly $230 million, was finally disclosed to the market, IndusInd Bank's shares plunged 27 percent. The episode triggered executive resignations, employee stock-option clawbacks, and a multi-agency reckoning that remains active across Mumbai's regulatory and judicial corridors well into 2026.

Warnings Inside, Silence Outside

According to SEBI's interim findings, the accounting shortfall in the bank's foreign-exchange derivatives portfolio did not materialize overnight. Internal email warnings had circulated among senior leadership detailing the mounting valuation gap well before retail and institutional investors learned that a sizable slice of the bank's net worth was at risk. Regulators allege that while those internal assessments were underway, the five named executives executed stock sales while in possession of confidential information that would predictably hammer the share price once released.

SEBI's May 28, 2025 interim order cited internal communications showing senior IndusInd Bank officials were aware of the foreign-exchange derivatives accounting hole before selling shares, prompting the regulator to impound 19.78 crore rupees in alleged avoided losses.

The Reserve Bank of India has monitored the prudential fallout at the lender, while the Serious Fraud Investigation Office has maintained an active probe in 2026 into the governance lapses that permitted the derivatives mismatch to accumulate undetected by public filings. Meanwhile, challenges to SEBI's interim directions have proceeded before the Securities Appellate Tribunal, where the former executives retain the right to contest the regulator's factual and legal conclusions.

The Cost of Going First

In corporate banking, governance crises are routinely described in the passive voice — discrepancies "emerge," controls "fall short," and valuations "require adjustment." What distinguished the IndusInd Bank affair in the eyes of regulators was the brisk personal decisiveness shown by executives who appeared considerably slower to share bad news with the market.

The resignations and ESOP clawbacks have begun addressing executive accountability inside the bank, even as the broader tribunal and SFIO proceedings continue without final adjudications. Yet the juxtaposition of numbers in SEBI's interim docket remains stark: ordinary shareholders absorbed a 27 percent cliff linked to a $230 million accounting crater, while insiders stood accused of stepping calmly off the ledge for $2.3 million in preserved value.
