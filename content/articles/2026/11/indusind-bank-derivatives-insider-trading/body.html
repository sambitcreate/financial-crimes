MUMBAI — When an Indian private bank finds a ₹2,000 crore ($230 million) hole inside its foreign-exchange derivatives book, shareholders expect fast disclosure. According to India’s securities regulator, five senior IndusInd Bank executives found time for one quicker chore first: selling their own stock.

In a May 28, 2025 interim order, SEBI accused former chief executive Sumant Kathpalia, senior executive Arun Khurana, and three colleagues of trading on unpublished price-sensitive information, impounding ₹19.78 crore ($2.3 million) in alleged avoided losses.

Warnings Inside, Silence Outside

SEBI’s order cites internal emails flagging the mounting derivatives shortfall well before investors ever heard about it. When the roughly ₹2,000 crore net-worth hit finally went public, IndusInd shares plunged 27 percent in a single selloff, triggering resignations and stock-option clawbacks.

The Reserve Bank of India has tracked the prudential fallout, while the Serious Fraud Investigation Office continues examining the governance breakdown in 2026. Meanwhile, the former executives are contesting SEBI’s interim order before the Securities Appellate Tribunal, and no final adjudication has been entered.

Bank disasters usually arrive wrapped in passive verbs — discrepancies “emerge” and valuations “require adjustment.” In SEBI’s interim docket, the contrast is sharper: ordinary investors rode a 27 percent cliff into a $230 million crater, while insiders stood accused of stepping off the ledge early for $2.3 million.
