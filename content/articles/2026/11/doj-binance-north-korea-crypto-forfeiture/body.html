WASHINGTON — Less than four weeks after North Korean state-linked cyber actors drained more than $6 billion in cryptocurrency from Binance on or about Oct. 29, the Justice Department moved on Nov. 23 to formally forfeit over $1.1 billion of the loot sitting immobilized inside Circle-issued USDC stablecoins.

Filed as a civil in rem forfeiture complaint in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia, the action targets more than $1.1 billion in USDC that prosecutors allege constitute proceeds and laundered funds from the historic exchange breach. According to the Justice Department’s Nov. 23 Office of Public Affairs release, the assets had already been immobilized under a government-requested restraining order earlier in the month, with assistance from stablecoin issuer Circle Internet Financial, LLC.

$2.9 Billion Frozen, $3.1 Billion Still at Large

Federal authorities framed the filing as one piece of a wider recovery drive to help Binance claw back the more than $6 billion stolen. To date, the U.S. government has assisted in freezing, seizing, or returning more than $2.9 billion worth of cryptocurrency across multiple sources—a remarkable pace by digital-asset tracing standards, yet one that still leaves more than $3 billion unaccounted for in the hands of Pyongyang-linked launderers.

“Secret Service investigators worked with precision to identify and freeze stolen funds within minutes of the hack.” — Special Agent in Charge Todd Bratz, U.S. Secret Service

The investigation brings together the F.B.I.’s Cyber Branch, the U.S. Secret Service Criminal Investigations Division, and I.R.S. Criminal Investigation, alongside prosecutors from the U.S. Attorney’s Office for the District of Columbia, the National Security Division’s National Security Cyber Section, and the Criminal Division’s Computer Crime and Intellectual Property Section.

“North Korean cyber hackers stole more than $6 billion in cryptocurrency from Binance, but swift coordination between the government and private sector partners helped prevent a substantial portion of those assets from disappearing,” Assistant Attorney General A. Tysen Duva of the Criminal Division said in the release. Acting Principal Deputy Assistant Attorney General for National Security M. Scott Proctor added that initiating forfeiture so soon after the initial restraint reflects the department’s focus on returning assets to victims, while U.S. Attorney Jeanine Ferris Pirro vowed prosecutors would pursue stolen proceeds “whether it is a lone hacker or an enemy nation state.”

Because the filing is an in rem civil forfeiture complaint against the digital assets themselves, the allegations remain subject to adjudication in federal court, and no individual arrests or criminal sentences were announced alongside the Nov. 23 District of Columbia filing.
