NEW YORK — Federal prosecutors in Manhattan and the Securities and Exchange Commission allege that the proprietary artificial intelligence powering the shopping startup Nate, Inc. — pitched to investors as automating 93 percent to 97 percent of online checkouts — actually operated at an automation rate near zero percent, relying instead on human workers in Manila and Romania to type orders by hand.

Albert Saniger, the founder and former chief executive of Nate, Inc., was criminally indicted in the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York and sued civilly by the S.E.C. on April 9, 2025. According to both the criminal indictment and the parallel civil regulatory complaint, Mr. Saniger raised more than $42 million from investors by portraying the company’s consumer shopping application as a breakthrough in autonomous neural-network commerce. His criminal trial is proceeding in 2026; Mr. Saniger is presumed innocent of the criminal charges unless and until proven guilty, and the S.E.C.’s allegations remain civil claims.

The parallel filings illustrate a pattern federal regulators have increasingly termed “AI-washing” — marketing conventional manual labor behind the vocabulary of frontier machine learning.

A 93-to-97 Percent Claim Meets a Zero-Percent Reality

According to prosecutors and the S.E.C., Nate, Inc.’s investor pitch depended on a crisp technological premise: that its universal checkout application used proprietary artificial intelligence to complete purchases seamlessly across e-commerce sites without human intervention. In fundraising presentations that brought in more than $42 million, investors were allegedly told the system achieved automation rates between 93 percent and 97 percent.

In reality, according to the indictment and civil complaint, the application’s true automated completion rate was approximately zero percent. Rather than executing transactions through a self-directed neural network, prosecutors allege, the company routed customer purchases to human operators working overseas in Manila and Romania, who manually typed payment and shipping details into merchant checkout forms behind the scenes.

Parallel criminal charges in the Southern District of New York and a civil S.E.C. complaint filed April 9, 2025, allege that Nate, Inc. raised more than $42 million while claiming 93 to 97 percent AI automation, even as its actual automation rate hovered near zero percent.

Criminal Trial and Civil Accountability

Because federal authorities brought coordinated actions on April 9, 2025, Mr. Saniger faces two distinct legal tracks. In the Southern District of New York, criminal prosecutors bear the burden of proving fraud beyond a reasonable doubt as the criminal trial proceeds in 2026. Separately, the S.E.C.’s civil enforcement suit seeks regulatory remedies under federal securities laws.

For venture investors eager to back the next wave of artificial-intelligence startups, the government’s allegations read like a dry parable of diligence skipped in haste. If prosecutors prove their case at trial, more than $42 million in venture capital funded a sleek consumer interface whose most reliable algorithm turned out to be a night shift in Manila and Romania.
