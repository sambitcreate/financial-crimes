In German banking law, “without undue delay” is supposed to mean hurry up. When a bank spots suspicious money moving through its wires, compliance alerts are meant to go straight to investigators. At J.P. Morgan SE, JPMorgan Chase’s Frankfurt hub, urgency ran on a gentler calendar — for an entire year.

In a binding administrative order issued Oct. 30, 2025, and announced Nov. 6, BaFin fined J.P. Morgan SE €45 million ($49 million) for systematically failing over twelve months to file Suspicious Transaction Reports on time. It was the largest anti-money-laundering fine Germany’s supervisor has ever handed down.

An All-Time Record That Lasts Eight Hours

Forty-five million euros sounds historic until you look up JPMorgan Chase’s income statement. Measured against the parent bank’s global profit engine, Germany’s sternest-ever laundering penalty evaporates in roughly eight hours of net income — about the length of a Frankfurt-to-New York flight plus baggage claim.

Notice what did not come with the record: zero criminal charges, zero individual sanctions, zero disruption to quarterly composure. Twelve months of systematic late filings isn’t an afternoon clerical slip; it is four consecutive quarters of operating posture.

BaFin works inside statutory caps it didn’t write, and issuing an agency record in Frankfurt is not nothing. Still, when Europe’s biggest laundering fine costs a megabank one shift’s profit, regulators haven’t priced systemic tardiness out of the market. They have simply published the menu price.
