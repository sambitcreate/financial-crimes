NEW YORK — You would think calling an investment firm Backward Hat Capital might serve as its own risk disclosure. Apparently not: more than 2,000 retail investors wired it roughly $600 million anyway.

In an indictment unsealed in November 2026, prosecutors in the Southern District of New York charged principals Phillip Williams and Devin Johnson with running a sprawling foreign-exchange Ponzi scheme. Both men are presumed innocent until proven guilty at trial.

According to the filing, the pitch was blunt: steady currency-trading gains exceeding 6 percent a month, or more than 100 percent a year. Foreign exchange is a brutal, razor-thin market where central banks and sovereign desks fight over basis points. Yet prosecutors allege Mr. Williams and Mr. Johnson marketed triple-digit annual returns like clockwork.

The Pocket-Calculator Problem

You do not need a quantitative risk desk to stress-test 6 percent monthly compounding. A $600 million pool doubling every year would not just beat every hedge fund in history; inside eighteen years, it would exceed the gross domestic product of planet Earth.

Prosecutors allege Backward Hat Capital solved that inconvenience the oldest way on Wall Street — by paying early withdrawals with fresh deposits from newer recruits while actual trading lagged far behind the glossy account statements.

The November unsealing ranks among the largest retail fraud indictments brought in Manhattan in recent years. A jury has yet to weigh the government’s evidence, and the defendants retain every trial right. Still, the docket offers one free lesson in asset allocation: whenever a currency fund promises to double your money every twelve months, the only thing reliably compounding is the indictment.
