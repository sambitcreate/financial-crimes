NEW YORK — After a three-month federal trial in Brooklyn, a jury convicted Huawei Technologies Co., Ltd. and three of its subsidiaries on Nov. 24 of bank fraud, wire fraud, money laundering conspiracy, conspiracy to steal trade secrets, and violations of U.S. sanctions on Iran.

The verdicts, returned in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of New York against Huawei, Chinese subsidiary Huawei Device Co. Ltd., and U.S. subsidiaries Futurewei Technologies Inc. and Huawei Device USA Inc., capped an eight-year prosecution over what the Justice Department’s Nov. 24 Office of Public Affairs release described as a decades-long campaign of deception. The jury returned guilty verdicts on 10 of 12 counts—including conspiracy to defraud the United States and substantive violations of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA)—while acquitting the corporate defendants on racketeering conspiracy and conspiracy to obstruct justice.

Backwards ‘ocsic’ Code, ‘Weihua’ Badges, and a Robot Arm in a Backpack

Prosecutors built their intellectual-property case around a catalog of corporate espionage that occasionally strained the limits of tradecraft. According to trial evidence summarized by the Justice Department, Huawei engineers who copied source code from Cisco Systems routers left Cisco’s internal code notes intact while attempting to disguise the provenance by spelling Cisco backwards as “ocsic”—noting in the files that portions remained as they existed in the “ocsic” code.

Elsewhere in the trial record, a Huawei employee caught photographing proprietary Fujitsu equipment wore a badge falsely identifying himself as an employee of “Weihua.” After signing a non-disclosure agreement with T-Mobile to access its controlled testing laboratory, a Huawei employee slipped a physical component of T-Mobile’s touchscreen-testing robot, “Tappy,” into his backpack and walked out. Prosecutors also proved that Huawei attached a confidential technical slide obtained under NDA from U.S. antenna maker Quintel directly to a Huawei patent application, and hired a university researcher to obtain hardware access from U.S. chip firm CNEX without disclosing he was acting for Huawei.

“For over two decades, Huawei built its global empire on a foundation of fraud, theft, and deception.” — Assistant Attorney General Tysen A. Duva, Justice Department Criminal Division

Skycom, Zaeim, and Billions in U.S.-Dollar Clearing

On the sanctions and banking counts, prosecutors showed that Huawei operated in Iran through a wholly owned subsidiary, Skycom, supplying turnkey internet-backbone and mobile-network services while helping Zaeim—a component of the Iranian intelligence apparatus—monitor and censor internet users, and providing surveillance cameras used to track protests. Huawei repeatedly told banks, journalists, and the U.S. government that Skycom was merely a business partner and that it had restricted its Iran business in 2011, statements the jury found were false and used to secure billions of U.S. dollars and U.S.-dollar clearing access.

Because only corporate entities stood trial in Brooklyn, no individual defendant faces prison time from the Nov. 24 verdict. U.S. District Judge Ann M. Donnelly scheduled sentencing for Sept. 14, 2027, when Huawei and its three subsidiaries face criminal fines, forfeiture, and restitution under the Eastern District of New York docket. Defense counsel has stated that the company intends to appeal.
