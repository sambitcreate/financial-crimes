LUXEMBOURG — The European Union's single market was built so goods could cross national borders with minimal bureaucratic friction. Under a cross-border tax scheme dismantled by European prosecutors in October 2026, however, crates of smartphones appear to have enjoyed a more lucrative itinerary than most European tourists — circling through eight member states and collecting value-added tax refunds at every stop.

Code-named "Operation Hermes," the enforcement sweep led by the European Public Prosecutor's Office, known as the EPPO, culminated on October 7, 2026, in more than 300 coordinated dawn raids across Portugal, Spain, Italy, Austria, France, Germany, Malta, and the Netherlands. Authorities detained 62 suspects linked to what prosecutors describe as two organized criminal syndicates operating a vast VAT carousel fraud. As of November 2026, pre-trial detentions and asset-freezing measures remain active across the participating jurisdictions.

Prosecutors estimate the scheme inflicted more than €410 million — roughly $445 million — in tax losses on European public treasuries. Alongside the arrests, investigators secured €310 million in formal asset-freezing orders and executed €9 million in physical seizures during the October raids, targeting the financial infrastructure behind the alleged carousel.

Spinning the Carousel

Value-added tax carousel fraud relies on a deceptively dry quirk of European Union trade law: cross-border business-to-business shipments within the bloc are generally zero-rated for VAT, meaning tax is supposed to be accounted for in the destination country. According to EPPO prosecutors, the two syndicates exploited that gap with industrial discipline, routing high-value consumer electronics — chiefly smartphones — through chains of shell wholesalers and "missing trader" entities that collected VAT on domestic sales and vanished before remitting a cent to tax authorities.

In announcing the October 7, 2026 raids under Operation Hermes, the European Public Prosecutor's Office alleged that two syndicates orchestrated an eight-country smartphone VAT carousel causing more than €410 million in public-revenue losses, prompting 62 detentions, €310 million in freezing orders, and €9 million in physical seizures.

Meanwhile, allied export companies further down the paper chain claimed legitimate VAT refunds from national treasuries when shipping the same consignments back across another border. In theory, a single batch of smartphones could complete lap after lap across Europe's highway and logistics corridors, generating fresh public-tax payouts on every revolution without ever landing in the pocket of an ordinary retail customer.

By the Numbers

  • €410M+ (~$445M): Estimated European Union VAT carousel tax losses alleged under Operation Hermes.
  • €310M / €9M: Value of freezing orders secured and physical assets seized by authorities.
  • 62 detained / 300+ raids: Suspects held across two alleged syndicates following Oct. 7, 2026 dawn raids.
  • 8 EU nations: Portugal, Spain, Italy, Austria, France, Germany, Malta, and the Netherlands.

A Continental Ledger Under Stress

Because the cases remain at the pre-trial stage in November 2026, all 62 detained individuals are presumed innocent until convicted in national courts. Even at this early procedural posture, however, Operation Hermes underscores why the European Union established a supranational prosecutor's office in the first place: national tax agencies auditing only their own border filings rarely see the full circle of a carousel until hundreds of millions of euros have already departed the treasury.

The freezing orders — covering €310 million against more than €410 million in estimated losses — represent an unusually assertive effort to recover public money before it dissipates. Still, the gap between €310 million in frozen claims, €9 million in cash and physical assets in hand, and €410 million in missing revenue illustrates the enduring economics of European tax fraud: moving imaginary paperwork across eight borders remains far faster than collecting it back.
