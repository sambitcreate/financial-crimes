BOISE — A federal judge in Idaho has entered final civil judgments against Michael A. Smith, the former president and chief operating officer of PetIQ, Inc., and his friend Douglas Joshua Dalton over insider trading ahead of private equity firm Bansk Group LP’s August 2024 acquisition of the pet-medication company.

According to Securities and Exchange Commission Litigation Release No. 26696, published Nov. 25, the U.S. District Court for the District of Idaho entered the consent judgments on Nov. 23 in SEC v. Michael A. Smith (No. 26-cv-00193), wrapping up a civil enforcement action filed March 31, 2026.

Ex-Wife’s Accounts and Call Options

The SEC alleged that Smith used material nonpublic information learned in PetIQ’s executive suite to purchase PetIQ common stock inside his ex-wife’s brokerage accounts before the Aug. 7, 2024 buyout announcement. Shortly after making those purchases, the agency alleged, Smith passed the impending acquisition news to Dalton, who loaded up on PetIQ call options.

When the Bansk Group deal went public, PetIQ shares surged 48 percent, generating combined illicit gains of $242,270.13—$145,754.69 attributable to Smith’s trades and $96,515.44 to Dalton’s options bets. Both men pleaded guilty to federal securities fraud in parallel Department of Justice prosecutions in the District of Idaho (United States v. Smith, No. 25-cr-00277, and United States v. Dalton, No. 26-cr-00048).

Forfeiture Offset and a Five-Year Boardroom Timeout

Under the final consent judgments, both Smith and Dalton are permanently enjoined from violating Section 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 and Rule 10b-5. The court ordered Smith liable for $145,754.69 in disgorgement and Dalton for $96,515.44—both sums deemed fully satisfied by forfeiture orders entered in their parallel criminal cases.

On top of the forfeited trading profits, the SEC imposed civil penalties of $25,000 each—$50,000 combined, or 20.6 percent of the illicit gains, far below the statutory treble-penalty ceiling available in insider-trading cases—and barred Smith from serving as a public-company officer or director for five years rather than permanently. The SEC credited assistance from the Justice Department’s White Collar and Corporate Enforcement Section, the U.S. Postal Inspection Service, and FINRA.
