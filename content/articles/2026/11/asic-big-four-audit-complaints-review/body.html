Australia’s four largest accounting partnerships—Deloitte, Ernst & Young, KPMG, and PwC—audit more than 94% of the country’s listed companies by market capitalisation and more than 85% of its registrable superannuation funds. They hold the keys to the nation’s retirement ledger. Yet, as Australia’s corporate watchdog calmly reminded the public on Nov. 25, it still does not actually license or regulate the firms themselves.

In Media Release 26-292MR and Report 848, the Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) published the results of a compulsory sweep of internal complaints logged by the Big Four between July 1, 2023, and June 26, 2026. Out of 558 responsive complaints handed over under statutory notices, ASIC identified 35 raising potential statutory audit misconduct or misuse of confidential audit client information.

Checked Boxes Without the Checks

Read the bullet list in Report 848 slowly. The internal complaints alleged audit procedures marked complete when testing never occurred; assets recorded in financial statements without verifying their existence; prior-year audit files recycled into current-year audits without quality checks; staff instructed to disregard or avoid escalating discrepancies; sampling manipulation; confidential client data misused; and retaliation against internal reporters who spoke up.

ASIC is careful—properly so—to emphasize that it did not investigate every allegation to a final finding, and that firm responses varied from disciplinary action to finding complaints unsubstantiated. Several matters have already been folded into existing inquiries, including ASIC’s ongoing investigation into KPMG over transparency-reporting misstatements, and individual names have been shared with CA ANZ, CPA Australia, and the Tax Practitioners Board.

Regulating the Mechanics, Not the Garage

The real punchline sits on page three of Report 848: “we register RCAs and regulate them in their conduct of statutory audits but we do not regulate audit firms generally,” ASIC writes, adding that “there are no regulatory provisions that directly govern how audit firms handle complaints.” Years after Australia’s parliamentary reckoning over Big Four governance and confidentiality scandals, the corporate regulator is still left “engaging with Treasury” to argue for basic firm-level licensing.

De-identifying 35 internal alarm bells across partnerships that sign off on 94% of a stock market is a useful diagnostic. Calling it oversight while the firms remain legally unlicensable is polite fiction.
