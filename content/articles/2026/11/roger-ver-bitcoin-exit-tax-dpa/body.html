There is a serene beauty to the deferred prosecution agreement Roger Ver — “Bitcoin Jesus” to early crypto devotees — signed with the Justice Department’s Tax Division in Los Angeles on Oct. 14, 2025. Federal prosecutors moved to dismiss a criminal indictment alleging $48 million in unpaid 2014 exit taxes in exchange for $49.9 million and precisely zero days in prison.

To be legally exact: Mr. Ver was not convicted. The Central District of California docket records no guilty verdict, just a remarkably tidy wire transfer eleven years after the tax year in question.

The Eleven-Year Carry Trade

For ordinary W-2 earners, the I.R.S. lacks this kind of imagination. Taxes leave your paycheck before you see them; willful evasion triggers compounding penalties, statutory interest, and real federal prison time. Almost no salaried worker gets invited to hold onto disputed funds for a decade and square up later for roughly four percent over principal.

Mr. Ver’s docket ran on a more accommodating calendar. Eleven years after his 2014 expatriation, the Justice Department accepted $1.9 million above the alleged $48 million tax bill, moved to dismiss the indictment, and asked for zero custody.

Prosecutors defend deferred prosecution agreements as efficient: take $49.9 million in cash and skip a messy international trial. Fine. Just notice what that efficiency teaches. With enough liquidity, a federal tax indictment doesn’t send you to prison — it turns into a decade-long line of credit.
