There is a serene beauty to the deferred prosecution agreement Roger Ver — “Bitcoin Jesus” to early crypto devotees — signed with federal prosecutors in Los Angeles and announced on Oct. 14, 2025. An indictment that once alleged at least $48 million in lost 2014 exit taxes was dismissed in exchange for $49.9 million and precisely zero days in prison.

To be legally exact: Mr. Ver was not convicted and did not plead guilty. In the agreement, he admitted that his 2016 expatriation returns left out some of his bitcoins, that the understatement was willful, and that it cost the Treasury $16,864,105. The Central District of California docket records no guilty verdict, just a remarkably tidy payment roughly a decade after the tax year in question.

The Eleven-Year Carry Trade

For ordinary W-2 earners, the I.R.S. lacks this kind of imagination. Taxes leave your paycheck before you see them; willful evasion triggers compounding penalties, statutory interest, and real federal prison time. Almost no salaried worker gets invited to hold onto disputed funds for a decade and square up later — tax, civil fraud penalty and interest bundled into one check — with the indictment dismissed.

Mr. Ver’s docket ran on a more accommodating calendar. Eleven years after his 2014 expatriation, the Justice Department accepted $49.9 million — the admitted $16.9 million tax loss, a civil fraud penalty of more than $12 million, and interest — and moved to dismiss the indictment, with zero custody.

Prosecutors defend deferred prosecution agreements as efficient: take $49.9 million in cash and skip a messy international trial. Fine. Just notice what that efficiency teaches. With enough liquidity, a federal tax indictment doesn’t send you to prison — it turns into a decade-long line of credit.
