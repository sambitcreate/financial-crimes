There is an instructive quietness to the deferred prosecution agreement that Roger Ver, long known in cryptocurrency circles as “Bitcoin Jesus,” reached with the Justice Department’s Tax Division in the Central District of California on Oct. 14, 2025. Under the agreement, federal prosecutors moved to dismiss a criminal indictment alleging approximately $48 million in unpaid 2014 expatriation taxes in exchange for a payment of $49.9 million — and precisely zero days in prison.

To be exact as a matter of law: Mr. Ver was not convicted of a crime. The Oct. 14, 2025 deferred prosecution agreement deferred and moved to dismiss the Los Angeles federal indictment rather than carrying the government’s allegations to a jury verdict. The public record therefore records no adjudication of guilt, only an extraordinarily tidy financial settlement more than a decade after the tax year at issue.

Still, even the most restrained reader of federal tax dockets may pause over the arithmetic. When an alleged $48 million exit-tax obligation from 2014 is resolved eleven years later for $49.9 million and no custodial sentence, the outcome invites a simple institutional question: how many American taxpayers would gladly subscribe to that financing schedule?

The Unofficial Expatriation Carry Trade

For ordinary W-2 wage earners, the Internal Revenue Service operates with an admirable lack of imagination. Taxes are withheld from every paycheck before the money ever reaches a checking account; substantial underpayments trigger compounding penalties, statutory interest and, in cases of alleged willful evasion, the realistic prospect of federal prison time. Very few salaried workers are invited to hold onto disputed funds for a decade and settle the matter later with a single check barely four percent above the alleged principal.

Mr. Ver’s docket in the Central District of California unfolded on an altogether different plane. Prosecutors had indicted him over roughly $48 million in alleged 2014 exit-tax evasion following his expatriation. Eleven years later, the Justice Department’s Tax Division accepted $49.9 million — an increment of roughly $1.9 million over the $48 million alleged tax figure — alongside a motion to dismiss the indictment and zero days of incarceration.

Under the Oct. 14, 2025 deferred prosecution agreement filed in the Central District of California with the Justice Department’s Tax Division, prosecutors moved to dismiss the indictment alleging roughly $48 million in 2014 exit-tax evasion upon a $49.9 million payment and zero prison time.

Justice by Invoice

Prosecutors routinely defend deferred prosecution agreements as pragmatic instruments that recover large sums for the Treasury without the expense, delay and litigation risk of a complex international tax trial. Collecting $49.9 million in cash is undeniably efficient, and federal courts cannot force prosecutors to try a case they have elected to resolve.

Yet efficiency carries its own quiet curriculum. When decade-old eight-figure tax indictments conclude with a wire transfer and a dismissed docket, the justice system teaches a lesson no press release intends to advertise: in white-collar tax enforcement, sufficient liquidity does not merely hire a formidable defense — it can convert a criminal indictment into a long-deferred invoice.
