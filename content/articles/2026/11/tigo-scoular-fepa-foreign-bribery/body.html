MIAMI — After a brief moratorium on corporate foreign-bribery prosecutions, the Justice Department’s Fraud Section has resumed active enforcement across Latin American supply chains — pairing discounted corporate settlements in Miami and Washington with the government’s first criminal charge under the Foreign Extortion Prevention Act in November 2026.

The revived docket reflects a narrower, distinctly border-conscious enforcement posture following the department’s post-Blanche Memo policy recalibration. Rather than pursuing routine commercial facilitation payments worldwide, federal prosecutors in the Southern District of Florida and the Justice Department’s Fraud Section have concentrated their foreign-bribery resolutions where illicit corporate payoffs intersect cartels, cash smuggling and cross-border trade corridors.

Two major corporate deferred prosecution agreements — covering TIGO Guatemala in November 2025 and The Scoular Company in July 2026 — laid out the terms of the new regime before prosecutors opened a fresh statutory front with the inaugural FEPA charge in November 2026.

Cash Bags, Cartel Links and a 50 Percent Discount

The largest corporate resolution of the post-moratorium cycle came in November 2025, when TIGO Guatemala entered a deferred prosecution agreement totaling $118.2 million. Federal filings tied the bribery scheme to bags of cash and a cartel nexus, placing the case squarely within the Justice Department’s recalibrated enforcement priorities.

Even with those stark factual markers, the settlement terms underscored how generously corporate cooperation is priced. TIGO Guatemala received a 50 percent discount off its penalty calculation and avoided the imposition of an independent compliance monitor — resolving its criminal exposure without a guilty conviction through the deferred prosecution structure.

Eight months later, in July 2026, the Justice Department announced a second corporate deferred prosecution agreement with The Scoular Company, totaling $10.18 million to resolve bribery connected to operations at the Mexican border. Together, the two resolutions confirmed that corporate FCPA enforcement had not disappeared so much as migrated south toward cross-border logistics and cartel-adjacent risk.

Through deferred prosecution agreements with TIGO Guatemala ($118.2 million, November 2025) and The Scoular Company ($10.18 million, July 2026), followed by the first Foreign Extortion Prevention Act criminal charge in November 2026, the DOJ Fraud Section and SDFL defined a supply-chain-focused bribery docket.

Opening the Demand-Side Ledger Under FEPA

The November 2026 milestone marked a structural expansion of federal authority. Whereas the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act historically targeted companies and individuals offering bribes abroad, the Foreign Extortion Prevention Act enables federal prosecutors to bring criminal charges directly against foreign officials accused of demanding or accepting bribes from American-linked businesses. As an initial criminal charge, the November 2026 FEPA prosecution remains an allegation pending judicial proceedings, and any accused individual is presumed innocent unless proven guilty.

For multinational compliance departments tracking Washington’s shifting enforcement memos, the combined ledger from Miami and the Fraud Section offered an unusually clear boundary line. Bribery enforcement had indeed resumed — provided cash bags, border crossings or cartels appeared somewhere in the supply chain.

By the Numbers

  • $10.18 million: The Scoular Company’s July 2026 deferred prosecution agreement resolving Mexican border bribery.
  • November 2026: Month the Justice Department brought its first criminal charge under the Foreign Extortion Prevention Act (FEPA); criminal charges remain pending allegations.
  • SDFL & DOJ Fraud Section: Primary federal venues driving the post-moratorium bribery docket.
