WASHINGTON — Federal banking and Treasury regulators announced a $1.21 billion civil enforcement settlement with Bank of America, N.A. on Oct. 13, 2026, concluding that one of the nation’s largest financial institutions committed willful anti-money-laundering lapses across billions of dollars in suspicious transactions — and then granting the bank a $750 million credit against its own penalty.

Under coordinated civil consent orders issued by the Treasury Department’s Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency and the Office of Foreign Assets Control, documented under Treasury release sb0657, the bank admitted to willful violations of the Bank Secrecy Act and federal anti-money-laundering rules. Regulators found that the institution failed to file timely Suspicious Activity Reports covering more than $5 billion in transactions linked to Chinese money-laundering networks and cross-border activity, alongside 475 separate federal sanctions violations.

On paper, the multi-agency reckoning carried headline-making figures. FinCEN assessed a $1.2 billion civil monetary penalty, while the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency imposed a $750 million penalty of its own and OFAC added a sanctions assessment. Stacked end to end, the combined assessments would have approached $1.96 billion.

The Architecture of a Multi-Agency Credit

In practice, Treasury’s interagency crediting framework functioned rather like a wholesale volume discount. Because regulators agreed to credit the comptroller’s $750 million penalty directly toward FinCEN’s $1.2 billion assessment, Bank of America’s net outlay settled at $1.21 billion — an amount comparable to roughly two weeks of the bank’s net income.

Even more notable to veterans of white-collar enforcement was the absence of individual accountability accompanying the corporate admissions. Unlike many Wall Street regulatory settlements resolved on a standard “without admitting or denying” basis, the Bank of America consent orders formally recorded admissions of willful laundering control failures. Yet the Oct. 13 enforcement package brought criminal charges against zero executives and zero bank officers.

Under the coordinated Treasury consent orders (release sb0657), federal regulators credited the $750 million Office of the Comptroller of the Currency assessment directly against FinCEN’s $1.2 billion penalty, reducing a stacked $1.96 billion enforcement tally to a $1.21 billion civil settlement with no individual prosecutions.

Civil Closure, No Criminal Docket

For compliance officers across the banking sector, the arithmetic was unmistakable. Late Suspicious Activity Reports exceeding $5 billion — filings meant to alert law enforcement to illicit funds moving through Chinese money-laundering networks and border corridors before those proceeds vanish — were met entirely through civil administrative remedies. The 475 sanctions lapses were folded into the same consolidated ledger.

Federal officials characterized the order as a landmark enforcement action under the Bank Secrecy Act. For shareholders watching from the sidelines, the mechanics of the settlement offered a quieter reassurance: even when a major American bank admits to willful anti-money-laundering failures measured in the billions, federal regulators remain willing to bundle the tab and leave the executive suite off the criminal docket.

By the Numbers

  • 475: Federal sanctions violations resolved under the Treasury enforcement package (release sb0657).
