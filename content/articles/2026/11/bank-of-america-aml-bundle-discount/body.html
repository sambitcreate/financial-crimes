WASHINGTON — Bank of America admitted on Oct. 13, 2026, to willful violations of federal anti-money-laundering law covering more than $5 billion in delayed alerts and 475 sanctions breaches. In return, Washington handed it a $750 million coupon.

The coordinated civil orders — issued by FinCEN, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, and OFAC under Treasury release sb0657 — look punishing only before you read the fine print. FinCEN levied a $1.2 billion civil money penalty. The OCC piled on $750 million. OFAC added a sanctions settlement. Stacked end to end, the tab reached $1.96 billion.

Then the interagency crediting kicked in. Regulators applied the OCC’s entire $750 million assessment straight against FinCEN’s bill, knocking Bank of America’s net outlay down to $1.21 billion — roughly fifteen days of corporate profit.

Willful Failures, Civil Receipts

What makes the package unusual isn’t the discount; wholesale crediting is practically a loyalty program for repeat megabank offenders. It is that Bank of America formally admitted willful Bank Secrecy Act violations rather than hiding behind the usual “neither admit nor deny” boilerplate.

According to Treasury’s orders, the bank sat on Suspicious Activity Reports covering more than $5 billion tied to Chinese money-laundering networks and cross-border cash flows. Those reports exist so investigators can trace illicit money before it clears the wire. Filing them late turns an early-warning system into a historical footnote.

Yet despite formal admissions of willful misconduct across billions of dollars and 475 sanctions lapses, the Oct. 13 orders stayed strictly administrative. Not one bank officer was indicted. Not one executive faced a personal fine.

In consumer banking, bouncing a check triggers an immediate fee with zero multi-agency credit. In institutional money laundering, rack up enough agencies at once and the government starts bundling the invoice — and politely leaves the handcuffs in the drawer.
