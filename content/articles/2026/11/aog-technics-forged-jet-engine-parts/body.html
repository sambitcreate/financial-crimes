LONDON — Modern commercial aviation is sustained by an obsessive paper trail: every bolt, seal, and turbine component inside a jet engine is supposed to carry an unbroken documentary lineage certifying that it is genuine and airworthy. For years, however, a single entrepreneur operating out of a spare bedroom in Surrey managed to infiltrate that global safety net, moving £39.3 million worth of jet engine parts backed by forged paperwork.

On February 23, 2026, at Southwark Crown Court in London, Jose Alejandro Zamora Yrala, the man behind AOG Technics, was sentenced to four years and eight months in prison following a prosecution by Britain's Serious Fraud Office. Mr. Zamora Yrala had pleaded guilty in December 2025, bringing a formal criminal conclusion to an investigation that rattled airlines, maintenance engineers, and aviation regulators across continents.

According to the Serious Fraud Office's case, AOG Technics supplied more than 60,000 jet engine components — generating approximately £39.3 million, or about $50 million — accompanied by falsified airworthiness release certificates, commonly known in aviation maintenance as Form 1 documents. When the forged documentation was uncovered, carriers around the world were forced to comb through maintenance logs and ground commercial aircraft to inspect and strip suspect parts from active engines.

A Global Supply Chain in a Spare Room

What made the fraud unusually unsettling to aviation executives was not the sophistication of an elaborate industrial syndicate, but the domestic banality of its headquarters. From an ordinary spare bedroom in Surrey, Mr. Zamora Yrala built a parts-brokerage operation that presented itself to major maintenance, repair, and overhaul providers as a credible link in the global aerospace supply chain. By fabricating the official release certificates meant to guarantee a part's provenance and safety testing, AOG Technics transformed unverified hardware into components trusted to operate at cruising altitude.

Prosecutors from the UK Serious Fraud Office established at Southwark Crown Court that AOG Technics distributed more than 60,000 jet engine parts valued at £39.3 million using forged airworthiness certificates, resulting in Mr. Zamora Yrala's December 2025 guilty plea and February 23, 2026 prison sentence.

In aviation, paperwork is not mere bureaucracy; it is the sole barrier separating certified aerospace engineering from guesswork. Because forged Form 1 certificates looked indistinguishable at a glance from authentic manufacturer releases, uncertified components were installed on commercial jetliners before discrepancies in serial histories triggered international safety alerts and grounded fleets.

The Arithmetic of Accountability

At Southwark Crown Court, the sentence of four years and eight months — 56 months in total — closed the criminal docket on a scheme that disrupted commercial aviation far beyond Surrey. Divided across the £39.3 million in tainted sales, the prison term works out to slightly more than £700,000 in falsified engine components for every month imposed, or well over a thousand suspect parts per month behind bars.

For global airlines that spent months dismantling engines to restore confidence in their maintenance registries, the AOG Technics prosecution offered a sobering lesson in modern outsourcing: a multibillion-dollar industry built on redundant engineering can still be brought to a halt by one man, a forged form, and a desk in a spare bedroom.
