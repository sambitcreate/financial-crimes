LONDON — Commercial aviation runs on an obsessive paper trail: every bolt and turbine blade inside a jet engine must carry a verified certificate proving it won’t fail at 36,000 feet. Working out of a spare bedroom in Surrey, one man infiltrated that global safety net and sold £39.3 million ($50 million) in jet engine parts backed by forged forms.

On Feb. 23, 2026, Southwark Crown Court sentenced AOG Technics director Jose Alejandro Zamora Yrala to four years and eight months in prison following a prosecution by Britain’s Serious Fraud Office. He had pleaded guilty in December 2025.

Sixty Thousand Parts, One Printer

Prosecutors showed that AOG Technics distributed more than 60,000 commercial engine components accompanied by fabricated Form 1 airworthiness release certificates, complete with fictitious staff profiles designed to make a suburban bedroom look like an international aerospace broker.

Because the forged certificates looked genuine at a glance, maintenance shops installed unverified hardware onto active commercial airliners. Once serial discrepancies surfaced, airlines worldwide had to ground planes and pull suspect components out of operational turbines.

Divided across 56 months in custody, the sentence works out to roughly £700,000 — and more than a thousand suspect engine parts — per month behind bars. Multibillion-dollar aerospace engineering, it turns out, can still be grounded by one man with a spare room and a convincing PDF.
