NEW YORK — Bradley Heppner, the former chairman of GWG Holdings and chief executive of Beneficient, was convicted on all criminal counts in Manhattan federal court on May 7, 2026, after a trial establishing that he looted more than $150 million from retail investors in a now-bankrupt public company — and after a federal judge ruled that his private consultations with an artificial-intelligence chatbot enjoyed no legal privilege.

The verdict in the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York, docketed as 25-Cr.-503 before Judge Rakoff, concluded a prosecution centered on the diversion of funds belonging to ordinary retail holders of GWG Holdings’ L-Bonds. Prosecutors proved that more than $150 million was siphoned out of the failing enterprise and routed into HCLP, a shell entity tied to Mr. Heppner, leaving retail bondholders to absorb the wreckage of GWG’s bankruptcy. Sentencing remains pending.

Yet even alongside the nine-figure scale of the investor losses, the case carved out a durable footnote in modern white-collar criminal law: an executive accustomed to retaining elite corporate defense attorneys discovered that an AI interface is not admitted to the bar.

An Unprivileged Digital Confidant

During the proceedings before Judge Rakoff, Mr. Heppner’s defense confronted the question of whether prompts he typed into Claude, an artificial-intelligence chatbot, could be shielded from prosecutors under the attorney-client privilege. In a closely watched ruling, the court held that those chatbot interactions were not privileged.

The legal distinction rests on a century-old principle that has proven remarkably resistant to Silicon Valley disruption. Attorney-client privilege protects confidential communications made to a licensed lawyer for the purpose of obtaining legal advice; typing queries into a commercial third-party AI platform satisfies neither the licensing requirement nor the confidentiality expectations of a legal retainer. While top-tier Manhattan defense counsel routinely bill $1,800 an hour precisely to maintain that statutory wall of secrecy, a chatbot subscription offers faster responses at a fraction of the cost — and, as Mr. Heppner learned, leaves behind an evidentiary record prosecutors can place before a jury.

In docket 25-Cr.-503, the Southern District of New York held that prompts submitted by a criminal defendant to a commercial AI chatbot are not protected by attorney-client privilege, leaving those digital exchanges exposed as ordinary discoverable evidence.

Retail Losses and Pending Sentencing

Beneath the novel technology ruling lay a familiar architecture of corporate self-dealing. GWG Holdings had raised capital from everyday retail L-Bond investors before tumbling into bankruptcy. Prosecutors demonstrated that rather than safeguarding those assets, Mr. Heppner directed more than $150 million through the shell entity HCLP, enriching interests under his control while public-company bondholders were left holding distressed claims.

With the May 7, 2026 conviction on all counts entered in Manhattan federal court, Mr. Heppner now awaits sentencing before Judge Rakoff. For corporate executives nationwide, the docket delivers two parallel reminders: diverting nine figures from retail investors into a shell entity remains a federal felony, and outsourcing legal strategy to an unprivileged chatbot is an unusually efficient way to document the paper trail.
