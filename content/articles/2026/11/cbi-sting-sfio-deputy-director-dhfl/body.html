NEW DELHI — India's Serious Fraud Investigation Office was designed to sit at the apex of the country's white-collar enforcement apparatus, tackling corporate collapses too complex and politically sensitive for ordinary policing. In October 2026, however, federal anti-corruption investigators arrived at a more awkward conclusion: one of the agency's own deputy directors was allegedly operating a private settlement desk on the side.

On October 12 and 13, 2026, the Central Bureau of Investigation arrested Harish Kumar Tanwar, an Indian Revenue Service officer serving as a Deputy Director with the SFIO, along with two other individuals, in a coordinated trap operation spanning New Delhi and Chennai. Prosecutors say the officer was caught red-handed as part of an alleged bribery arrangement tied directly to the agency's multi-thousand-crore investigation into Dewan Housing Finance Corporation Limited, known as DHFL. Following the arrests, the accused were produced before the Rouse Avenue Court in New Delhi and remanded in custody.

According to the CBI, Mr. Tanwar had allegedly demanded a bribe of 1 crore rupees — roughly $115,000 — in exchange for shielding an accused party from the full weight of the ongoing DHFL corporate fraud inquiry. Investigators said they seized 20 lakh rupees, or approximately $23,000, during the sting operation as a partial installment of the demanded sum.

An Enforcement Matryoshka

The case has given New Delhi's financial bar a grimly symmetrical tableau: a senior investigator tasked with unwinding one of India's most notorious multi-thousand-crore financial collapses now sitting in custody after being snared by another federal agency. Inside the nested architecture of Indian corporate enforcement, DHFL was supposed to represent the state's determination to police systemic shadow-banking fraud. Instead, the CBI's allegations suggest that the investigative file itself had been treated as a negotiable instrument.

According to the Central Bureau of Investigation's court remand filings at Rouse Avenue Court, investigators executed a trap operation across New Delhi and Chennai on October 12–13, 2026, seizing 20 lakh rupees of an allegedly demanded 1-crore-rupee bribe meant to provide protection in the DHFL probe.

As with all criminal arrests, the CBI's allegations remain subject to trial, and Mr. Tanwar and his two co-accused are presumed innocent unless proven guilty in court. The remand proceedings before Rouse Avenue Court mark the early custodial phase of a case investigators say spans New Delhi and Chennai.

The Price of Regulatory Capture

What has drawn particular attention among legal observers is not merely the breach of public trust alleged against an Indian Revenue Service officer on deputation to the country's premier corporate-fraud unit, but the sheer modesty of the alleged discount. Measured against the multi-thousand-crore scale of the DHFL collapse, a 1-crore-rupee demand — with 20 lakh rupees changing hands up front — reads less like grand larceny than an astonishingly cheap tollbooth installed along a highway of institutional failure.

For the SFIO, whose credibility depends on convincing courts and creditors that its forensic files are insulated from influence, the sting delivers an unwelcome audit of its own corridors. In the business of policing white-collar crime, it turns out, the hardest ledger to verify is sometimes the one on the investigator's own desk.
