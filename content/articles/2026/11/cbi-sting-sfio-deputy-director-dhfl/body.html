NEW DELHI — India’s Serious Fraud Investigation Office exists to unwind corporate collapses too big for ordinary police. In October 2026, federal anti-corruption agents concluded one of its own deputy directors was allegedly running a side window for private relief.

On Oct. 12 and 13, the Central Bureau of Investigation arrested Harish Kumar Tanwar — an Indian Revenue Service officer on deputation as an S.F.I.O. deputy director — along with two others in a coordinated trap across New Delhi and Chennai. Rouse Avenue Court remanded them in custody.

According to the C.B.I., Mr. Tanwar demanded ₹1 crore (about $115,000) to shield an accused party inside the S.F.I.O.’s multi-thousand-crore inquiry into Dewan Housing Finance Corporation Limited, known as DHFL. Agents say they caught him red-handed with a ₹20 lakh ($23,000) first installment. All three defendants remain presumed innocent pending trial.

A Discount Tollbooth on a Billion-Dollar Collapse

The arrest hands New Delhi’s corporate bar a grim Matryoshka doll: a federal anti-corruption bureau arresting a senior investigator from the federal corporate-fraud bureau over a case about a collapsed shadow bank.

What jumps off the remand sheet isn’t just the alleged breach of trust, but the price tag. Against a multi-thousand-crore DHFL collapse, a ₹1 crore bribe demand — with ₹20 lakh paid up front — reads less like grand larceny than an astonishingly cheap tollbooth installed along a highway of institutional wreckage.

For the S.F.I.O., the sting is an uncomfortable internal audit: in the business of policing white-collar crime, the hardest ledger to verify is sometimes the one sitting on the investigator’s own desk.
