NEW DELHI — India’s Serious Fraud Investigation Office exists to unwind corporate collapses too big for ordinary police. In October 2026, federal anti-corruption agents accused one of its own deputy directors of running a side window for private relief.

In mid-October, the Central Bureau of Investigation arrested Harish Kumar Tanwar — an Indian Revenue Service officer posted as an S.F.I.O. deputy director and investigating officer in Chennai — along with a Delhi advocate, Bharat Sharma, and his relative Aryan Sharma, after laying a trap.

According to the C.B.I., as reported by The Hindu and other outlets, ₹1 crore (about $115,000) was demanded from a complainant in return for favorable treatment in the S.F.I.O.’s inquiry linked to Dewan Housing Finance Corporation Limited, known as DHFL. The agency says the advocate accepted ₹20 lakh ($23,000) as a first installment on the officer’s behalf. None of the allegations has been tested in court, and all three accused are presumed innocent.

A Discount Tollbooth on a Billion-Dollar Collapse

The arrest hands New Delhi’s corporate bar a grim Matryoshka doll: a federal anti-corruption bureau arresting a senior investigator from the federal corporate-fraud bureau over a case about a collapsed shadow bank.

What jumps off the case file isn’t just the alleged breach of trust, but the alleged price tag. Against a multi-thousand-crore DHFL collapse, an alleged ₹1 crore demand — with ₹20 lakh allegedly paid up front — reads less like grand larceny than an astonishingly cheap tollbooth installed along a highway of institutional wreckage.

For the S.F.I.O., whatever the courts eventually decide, the sting is an uncomfortable internal audit: in the business of policing white-collar crime, the hardest ledger to verify is sometimes the one sitting on the investigator’s own desk.
