GURUGRAM, India — Public climate loans are supposed to put electric cabs on polluted roads. At Gensol Engineering and its ride-hailing affiliate BluSmart, regulators found a slice of that state credit took a scenic detour straight into DLF The Camellias — Gurugram’s most coveted address — plus a set of golf clubs.

Two state lenders, the Indian Renewable Energy Development Agency (IREDA) and Power Finance Corporation (PFC), extended ₹977.75 crore (roughly $114 million) in electric-vehicle fleet loans to Gensol. On July 30, 2025, the Securities and Exchange Board of India confirmed sweeping market and directorship bans against promoters Anmol and Puneet Jaggi.

Following the Green Rupee

SEBI’s confirmatory order traced ₹42.94 crore ($5 million) of those fleet-loan proceeds out through an EV dealer, Go-Auto, and onward via Capbridge Ventures to finance a luxury apartment at DLF The Camellias, alongside personal spending including golf clubs from accounts earmarked for passenger cabs.

By late 2026, the collapse has spread across five Indian forums: insolvency proceedings at the National Company Law Tribunal, appeals at the Securities Appellate Tribunal, and active probes by both the Enforcement Directorate and the Serious Fraud Investigation Office.

No criminal convictions have been entered, and the Enforcement Directorate and S.F.I.O. inquiries remain ongoing. Even so, the public regulatory trail already tells a tidy green-finance story: borrow ₹978 crore from taxpayers to electrify urban transit, and park $5 million of it in Gurugram’s priciest driveway.
