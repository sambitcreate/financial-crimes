GURUGRAM, India — State-backed climate finance in India is intended to accelerate the transition away from combustion engines, ideally by putting electric cabs onto congested urban roads. According to securities regulators, however, a portion of the public lending extended to Gensol Engineering and its affiliated ride-hailing venture, BluSmart, took a scenic detour — coming to rest in an ultra-luxury residence at DLF The Camellias in Gurugram, accompanied by an assortment of golf clubs.

The unraveling of Gensol Engineering and the electric-fleet ecosystem built by its promoters, Anmol Jaggi and Puneet Jaggi, has evolved into one of India's most closely watched corporate governance failures. On July 30, 2025, the Securities and Exchange Board of India confirmed sweeping bans barring the brothers from the securities markets and from holding company directorships. By late 2026, the fallout has expanded well beyond the stock exchange, with insolvency proceedings advancing before the National Company Law Tribunal alongside active investigations by both the Enforcement Directorate and the Serious Fraud Investigation Office.

At the center of the regulatory record is 977.75 crore rupees — roughly $114 million — in electric-vehicle loans sanctioned by two state-owned lenders, the Indian Renewable Energy Development Agency (IREDA) and Power Finance Corporation (PFC). The funds were earmarked to finance electric fleets meant to anchor India's green mobility push. Instead, regulators found that corporate loan proceeds were systematically commingled and diverted away from their stated purpose.

Following the Green Rupee

Regulatory orders trace a remarkably direct itinerary beneath the green-energy branding. According to SEBI's findings, 42.94 crore rupees — approximately $5 million — of the loan proceeds was routed through an intermediary dealer, Go-Auto, and onward via Capbridge to finance a luxury apartment at DLF The Camellias in Gurugram. Investigators also flagged personal expenditures, including golf clubs, funded from corporate accounts meant for electric passenger vehicles.

Confirming market and directorship bans on July 30, 2025, SEBI found that promoter-controlled entities diverted state electric-vehicle loans from IREDA and PFC through Go-Auto toward a DLF The Camellias apartment and personal purchases.

The gap between the stated mission and the documented ledger proved hard to soften. BluSmart had positioned itself as a zero-emission alternative to conventional ride-hailing apps, relying on public-sector green credit to expand its fleet. Confirming SEBI's bans stripped the founders of executive authority even as proceedings continued before the Securities Appellate Tribunal and creditors moved to salvage what remained.

Insolvency and Multi-Agency Scrutiny

With the matter now spanning five distinct regulatory, judicial, and investigative fronts in 2026, the case has become a cautionary exhibit for India's state lenders. The NCLT insolvency process is attempting to untangle assets and liabilities across the damaged corporate group, while the Enforcement Directorate and the Serious Fraud Investigation Office continue examining whether the diversion of state credit crossed into broader statutory offenses.

None of the criminal or money-laundering inquiries have yet resulted in final court convictions. Yet the arithmetic already recorded in public regulatory orders leaves little ambiguity about how a green-transit balance sheet ended up financing Gurugram's most coveted driveway.
