MUMBAI — Jane Street built one of Wall Street’s most profitable trading machines by saying almost nothing in public. In Mumbai, India’s market regulator decided silence isn’t a compliance strategy — and slapped a ₹4,843.57 crore (about $553 million to $565 million) cover charge on the firm’s options desk.

In a July 2025 interim order, the Securities and Exchange Board of India barred four Jane Street trading affiliates from Indian markets and ordered the sum impounded as unlawful gains from expiry-day derivatives trades. The firm wired the money into escrow and appealed to the Securities Appellate Tribunal. On Oct. 21, 2026, after months of order-book sparring, the tribunal reserved its final ruling.

The Expiry-Day Seesaw

The fight centers on weekly expiration days in Bank Nifty and Nifty 50 derivatives, where Indian retail traders pile into short-dated options between 9:15 a.m. and 3:30 p.m. in volumes unmatched anywhere on earth.

SEBI alleges the four Jane Street affiliates did not simply provide liquidity. According to the interim order, they bought aggressively across underlying cash stocks and futures early in the session, stacked huge short-option bets near the peak, and unwound the cash leg in the afternoon to steer index expiry levels in their favor.

Jane Street disputes that reading, arguing its trading was lawful market-making rather than manipulation. No criminal charges have been filed, and the tribunal’s pending order will decide whether SEBI’s interim impoundment stands.

For years, Western quant shops treated Mumbai’s derivatives boom like a low-friction ATM where microsecond speed reliably separated New York models from domestic retail flow. Whatever the tribunal rules, SEBI’s half-billion-dollar freeze already hit Jane Street where it hates paying most: forcing Wall Street’s quietest partners to explain their trades out loud.
